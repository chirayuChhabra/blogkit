Gravity: From Newton to Einstein

A journey through the mechanics of the cosmos. Discover how our understanding of gravity evolved from an invisible tether to the curvature of spacetime.

The Invisible Tether

For most of human history, gravity wasn't considered a universal force. Aristotle believed that objects simply moved toward their "natural place"—heavy objects fell because they belonged to the earth, while smoke rose because it belonged to the air. The heavens, governed by entirely different laws, were thought to be perfect and unchanging.

This paradigm shattered in 1687 when Isaac Newton published his Principia. Newton's great insight was unification: the very same force that pulls an apple to the ground is the force that keeps the moon in orbit around the Earth. Gravity is universal.

Newton described this attractive force elegantly. It grows stronger as the masses increase, and it weakens dramatically as the distance between the objects grows (following an inverse-square law).

Important

While Newton's math was incredibly precise, it had a glaring conceptual flaw: it assumed gravity acted instantaneously across vast distances, with no mechanism for how the force was transmitted.

Checkpoint: Newtonian Physics

If you double the distance between two planets, what happens to the gravitational force between them?

Newton's law is an inverse-square law (1/r21/r^21/r2). Doubling the distance (22=42^2 = 422=4) reduces the force to one-quarter.

What was Newton's major conceptual breakthrough regarding gravity?

Newton unified the heavens and the earth, realizing the force pulling an apple is the same force pulling the moon.

The Fabric of Spacetime

In 1915, Albert Einstein completely rewrote the rules of the universe with his General Theory of Relativity. He realized that space and time are not a static stage where events take place; they are a dynamic, interwoven fabric called spacetime.

Massive objects—like stars and planets—warp this fabric. When an object moves through space, it simply travels along the straightest possible path in this curved geometry.

Imagine placing a heavy bowling ball on a trampoline; it creates a deep depression. If you roll a marble across the trampoline, its path curves around the bowling ball, not because an invisible string is pulling it, but because the surface it's rolling on is curved.

Note

Einstein's General Relativity didn't just fix the 'instantaneous' problem—it predicted entirely new phenomena, like black holes and gravitational waves, which we now observe regularly.

Final Review

According to General Relativity, why does the Earth orbit the Sun?

In General Relativity, massive objects warp spacetime, and other objects simply follow the straightest path (geodesic) through that curved geometry.
